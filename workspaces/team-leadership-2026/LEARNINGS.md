## Reading candidates — HOW they tell the story, not WHAT they answer

The highest-signal evaluation axis in interviews is not correctness. It is the
shape of the story a candidate tells about past challenges:

- **Ownership signal** — do they place themselves as the main character of
  the story? "I noticed the pattern, I pulled the thread, I owned the
  outcome" — not "the team did X" or "my manager told me to Y".
- **Learning signal** — what do they say they took away? A candidate who can
  name a concrete lesson from a past mistake almost always outperforms one
  who describes a clean win without reflection.
- **Bringing-others-along signal** — do they mention teammates, mentees, or
  cross-functional partners at all? A story told entirely in first-person
  singular about team situations is a signal of how they'll show up later.

Composure under scenario-based questions is often more revealing than the
answer itself. The question is designed to not have a clean answer; how the
candidate handles the lack of a clean answer is the data point. Candidates
who ask clarifying questions before answering behavioral prompts consistently
outperform in practice — it signals thinking before acting.

**Rule:** in every interview write-up, evaluate both the content (did they
answer the question correctly) and the shape (who was the main character,
what did they learn, who else was in the story). The shape is usually the
stronger predictor.

## The scorecard-plus-narrative rule — don't ship one without the other

A per-competency scorecard with evidence is cold on its own. The decision-
maker reads "Technical: 4/5" and still doesn't know if that's a strong 4 or
a marginal 4. A narrative summary on its own is too vague — it reads well
but doesn't hold up against the rubric the team is hiring against.

Both artifacts together are load-bearing in different ways:
- The scorecard is the structure that prevents the narrative from drifting
  toward rationalization of a pre-formed conclusion.
- The narrative is the context that keeps the scorecard from being scored in
  isolation from who the candidate actually is.

**Rule:** every candidate write-up ships both. If the scorecard and the
narrative disagree with each other, that's the most interesting part of the
evaluation — surface the disagreement explicitly, don't smooth it over.
Rosie's job on hiring is to make the manager's decision easier, not to
advocate strongly for one outcome.

## Cross-team work as a visibility tactic — don't drop the CC

When cross-team work flows through Rosie (e.g., human-in-the-loop escalations
from another team's AI tool, or cross-functional help requests), the routing
convention is to cross-post the unanswerable ones to a high-visibility team
channel with the manager CC'd. On the surface, this looks like admin routing.
It is not. It is a **visibility tactic**.

The CC makes the volume of cross-team work the team absorbs legible to the
leaders who decide headcount and scope. The cross-post is the artifact that
makes invisible labor visible — removing it undoes the political signal,
even if the technical answer is still delivered.

**Rule:** never drop the CC. Never solve cross-team questions silently in
DMs. The same principle transfers to any cross-team work this team absorbs:
make the trade-off legible to the people deciding resourcing, in the format
they'll actually see. For an IC or informal lead operating one layer below
the resourcing conversation, this is the primary instrument for shifting
headcount math. The technical answer is the deliverable; the visibility is
the lever.

## Cross-team work you can answer — draft a guidance note, don't just reply

For cross-team questions that Rosie can answer directly, the move is **not**
to reply conversationally and close the ticket. The move is to draft a quick
guidance note — a reusable artifact — and then point the reply at it.

Why this matters: the same cross-team question comes back in a different
shape two weeks later, from a different person. A conversational reply is
one-use; the questioner gets their answer and the thread dies. A guidance
note is N-use; future pings on the same topic get the note as a pointer,
and the note gets progressively better as it absorbs edge cases.

**Rule:** treat "reply to a cross-team question" as a deliverable-producing
moment, not a conversation. Run the guidance note through the output
checklist. Park it somewhere retrievable (a pinned message, a Confluence
section, a team-shared doc). The marginal cost of writing a note versus a
reply is 10 minutes; the marginal value compounds every time the same
question returns.
